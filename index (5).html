Independent research program · Neurotechnology for anxiety

Anxiety is a loop you can't hear yourself enter.

We're here to help you hear it.

We are building the device that hears it for you — a quiet tap on the wrist within a second of the loop starting, long before you would have caught it yourself. Not reading your thoughts. Interrupting the spiral that feeds on them.

01 — What we're doing

A prosthetic for noticing

Worry is not a thought you have. It is a loop you are inside, and the defining feature of being inside it is that you cannot tell.

By the time most people notice they have been spiralling, they have lost twenty minutes — sometimes a night.

There is a reason the loop is so sticky. Verbal worry damps the body's fear response, so it delivers real relief in the moment while quietly preventing that fear from resolving. It feels like problem-solving and works like avoidance. That is why willpower fails against it, and why telling someone to stop worrying has never once helped anybody.

The intervention is small and physical. A light EEG band reads the signature of self-referential absorption. When the loop starts, you get a tap on the wrist within a second — no words, no transcript, no judgement. Just: you're in it. You surface, and go back to what you were doing.

Repeat that a few thousand times and something changes. Your own attention system learns the signature, because the cue kept pairing an external signal with an internal state you could not previously feel. The device is designed to become unnecessary.

This is also the most responsible thing a brain-computer interface can do here. Reading inner speech needs surgery, works on barely a third of mental moments, and fails completely for people who don't think in words. Detecting the state needs none of that.

02 — The mechanism

Why observing a thought works

The intervention is observation. Not fighting the thought, not replacing it, not pushing it away — just seeing it, clearly, as a thought.

That sounds too simple to be a mechanism. It is also the best-evidenced one in this field. The technical name is decentering: holding a worry as a passing mental event rather than a true thing you are obliged to solve. Registering "I am having the thought that I've ruined this" instead of "I've ruined this."

The shift is small. The consequence is not — increases in decentering precede and predict symptom reduction across mindfulness-based therapy, CBT and acceptance-based treatment.

What changes, and in what order. First the thought loses its authority: you can see it without being moved by it. Then the loop gets shorter, because what was keeping it running was you being inside it. Then, with enough practice, the loops come less often and the distress attached to them falls away. Awareness is not a trick that makes a worry vanish in the moment. It is the thing that stops feeding it — and an unfed loop does fade.

What does not work is forcing it to vanish. That is the one approach with evidence squarely against it: across 31 studies of deliberate thought suppression, suppressed thoughts returned stronger once the effort stopped. You don't get rid of a worry by refusing it. You get rid of its grip by watching it.

Which is why the device taps your wrist instead of telling you what you're thinking. It restores the one thing the loop takes from you: the chance to look at it.

03 — Evidence

What the published record supports

Why we decode state, not words

Error rates for turning brain activity into language. Lower is better; bars show the reported range across participants and vocabulary sizes. Even the best invasive result gets roughly one word in ten wrong, and the portable non-invasive option is unusable for content. That gap is the entire argument for reading state instead.

Willett 2023 · Kunz 2025 · Brain2Qwerty 2025. WER for word-level results, CER for typed sentences.
Mechanism

Observing changes the grip

Decentering precedes and predicts symptom reduction across three treatment families. It is the active ingredient — not relaxation, not suppression.

Already readable

The signal exists today

Real-time fMRI of the posterior cingulate separates undistracted awareness from distracted, efforting awareness in meditators' own moment-to-moment reports.

Training effect

It builds a better detector

One month of training increased connectivity between the default-mode, salience and executive networks. People got better at catching the lapse — not at having fewer thoughts.

Honest caveat

Nobody has beaten sham

Pooled across eight randomised controlled trials, attention neurofeedback showed no significant effect against sham. Our Phase 2 is built to clear that bar — or to report that it didn't.

04 — The problem with mind-reading

Most of a mind isn't words

Interrupt people at random and ask what was in their experience a moment ago, and words turn out to be the minority of it. This is the finding that rules out transcription as a target and rules in state detection.

Share of randomly sampled moments containing each kind of experience

295 samples from 30 people. Categories overlap, so they don't sum to 100%. The striking part isn't the average but the spread: inner speech ranged from 0% to 75% between individuals. Some people never experience an inner voice at all.

Heavey & Hurlburt 2008, Descriptive Experience Sampling.

Unsymbolised thinking is the category that breaks a decoder. A fifth of sampled moments contain definite thought content with neither words nor images in it. There are no phonemes to classify and no picture to reconstruct. Point a modern language-model decoder at that and it will produce fluent, confident, invented sentences — and attribute them to the person.

05 — How it breaks

One mechanism, two failures

Inner speech is a motor simulation of articulation. It emits a content-specific prediction of how it would sound, and that prediction is what tags the utterance as yours. Two different faults in that one system produce two very different experiences.

Worry is a termination failure with the self-tag intact. A voice is a tagging failure. Different faults, different interventions — and a reason not to treat them as one problem.
06 — The prediction

Shorter loops, not fewer

The mechanism predicts that dwell time falls while the number of episodes stays roughly flat. That is why our primary endpoint is dwell, not silence — and why a trial measuring "calmness" would miss the effect entirely.

07 — The plan

Four phases, three gates

Each phase is independently publishable and gated on the one before it. No new surgical implantation occurs at any stage — the intracranial phase works with patients already implanted for clinical reasons.

08 — Where we draw the line

What we will not build

Neural data became a regulated category in 2024. These are commitments, not aspirations, and they are in the paper where a review board can hold us to them.

No content, ever

The system reports how long you dwelled and how fast you returned. It never reconstructs a word. Content decoding is not a feature we have postponed — it is one the architecture excludes.

The output belongs to the wearer

Measurements go to the person they came from. No third-party-facing inference about individuals, and no employment, education or insurance applications.

Sham-controlled from day one

Every interventional study carries a yoked sham arm, matched for cue rate and reinforcement. A result that can't beat sham gets published as a null, not reframed.

No new implantation

Our intracranial work happens with patients already implanted for clinical reasons and consented separately for research. We put electrodes in nobody.

Stopping rules in advance

The paper names four results that would end the program. They're pre-registered so that we can't quietly move them later.

09 — Questions

The things people ask first

Is this mind reading?

No, and the distinction is architectural rather than a promise. A mind-reading system decodes content — the words. This one decodes state — whether you are currently absorbed in self-referential thought, how long you've been there, how fast attention returns. It can tell that you are lost in thought. It cannot tell what the thought is, and it is not built in a way that could be extended to.

Does it work yet?

No. Nothing has been collected. Phase 0 — reanalysis of two public EEG datasets — has not begun. Everything on this page is either published evidence from other people's work, or a stated prediction with a threshold attached. The honest status is: a well-specified plan with a clear first experiment and no results.

Why anxiety and not schizophrenia?

Because the mechanisms are different and the evidence is unequal. Worry is self-authored inner speech whose loop fails to terminate. Voices are experienced as not-yours, and meta-analysis finds no significant Broca's or Heschl's activity during them. Anxious inner speech has the strongest mechanism evidence, the lowest risk, and the clearest regulatory path. If a trainer can't beat sham in healthy anxious adults, it will not help anyone with a psychotic disorder. The harder populations have to be earned.

Isn't this just a meditation app with extra steps?

Fair challenge, and the answer is latency. Meditation teaches you to notice you've drifted — but the noticing arrives seconds to minutes late, which is a weak learning signal. A detector firing under a second converts delayed reinforcement into near-immediate reinforcement. That, not mind-reading, is the plausible mechanism by which hardware could speed up what practice does slowly. If the sham-controlled trial says otherwise, that claim dies.

Who is behind this?

One person, currently: Aagat Adhikari. This is an independent research program, not a company and not a lab. The work it describes needs collaborators with facilities, clinical partners and grant access — which is what the collaborate section is for. Nothing here claims institutional backing it does not have.

Partners, more than anything else. Phase 0 runs on public data and a laptop. Everything after it needs people and rooms we don't have yet.

Most needed

An epilepsy monitoring unit

Phase 3 depends on a clinical partner running stereo-EEG monitoring with an active cognitive-neuroscience program. Conversations started early cost nothing.

EEG lab access

High-density EEG for twenty participants, plus autonomic recording for the temporal-precedence sub-study.

Phase 1

Experience-sampling expertise

Descriptive Experience Sampling needs multi-day iterative training of participants. We want someone who has run it properly.

There are no empirical data on the phenomenology of inner signing. The hand-area decoding literature predicts something testable here.

12 — Why this exists

Hearing Silence

Aagat Adhikari

The sky was pouring. I was soaked, exhausted, and drowning. My head was loud, as if there were multiple people inside my head. On top of that, my electric scooter stopped working far from home — maybe because of the rain, or maybe for something else. I didn't want to be anywhere. I looked up at the sky. Then I looked to my left. There was a church. I walked in, not looking for God, but for a quiet room to sit down in.

I sat down, head in my hands, and let it all out. Then I heard footsteps, then more and more. It was time for worship. I tried to hold it in and stay invisible, but a soaked, crying man who clearly didn't "look" Christian is hard to miss. Then the music started. Everyone started praying, and before I knew it, I was praying with them. The tears didn't stop, but within minutes I was no longer crying. Every drop felt like relief. After a few clumsy tries, making the sign of the cross felt natural.

After the prayers, the father spoke about young people who come to the country with big dreams, get overwhelmed when things don't go their way, and despite all this, keep going. Somehow, he saw what my problems were really about. He even arranged for that day's donations to be given to me, but I had not come there for pity. So as the ushers were busy collecting, I quietly slipped out.

I was embarrassed — a grown man crying in front of a lot of strangers — but I had never felt so light. The music, the lights, and the highs and lows of the synchronized chants had taken me into another world. When I stepped outside, there was only me. I felt like I had been deaf my entire life before that.

I tried the scooter again. It started. I rode home with two questions: Who is God? And where did those voices come from, and where did they go?

So I started chasing the first question by reading. I bought an Old Testament Bible. One day, as I was reading it in public, a man with long hair and a thick beard offered me the New Testament. I thanked him and said I'd read it next. He gave it to me and said, "Jesus will help us." I thought, He already has. Days later I opened it and found a hundred-dollar bill on the first page. I had left the church so that I wouldn't take anything, but grace found me anyway.

That second question led me to the brain. I learned that for millions of people, the noise I felt that night never stops. For people with schizophrenia or severe anxiety, the voices never fade. Not everyone finds a quiet church on the right day. I was lucky, but luck isn't a treatment.

That's why I'm drawn to brain-computer interfaces and AI decoders that are being developed rapidly by big tech like Meta. I aim to study how we can calm the brain activity behind hallucinations. I want other people to finally hear silence too.

I go to church often now, but only on sunny days. I have found friends committed to Jesus, and I notice how people who are kind, respectful, calm, and above all, graceful come to church. Faith gave me peace. Science might help me share it with the world.

Amen.
